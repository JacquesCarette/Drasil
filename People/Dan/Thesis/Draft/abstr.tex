\prefacesection{Abstract}

Scientific software is represented by more than executable code. Requirements,
designs, source code, and build instructions present overlapping knowledge to
different audiences. Repetition across these artifacts is often necessary, but
independently editable occurrences can become inconsistent, incomplete, or
untraceable. This fragmentation increases maintenance effort and allows
documented models and implementations to diverge, undermining confidence in the
correctness and reproducibility of scientific results. This thesis investigates
whether capturing shared knowledge explicitly and generating artifact-specific
presentations can reduce that burden.

Drawing on six \mbox{\emph{input~$\rightarrow$~process~$\rightarrow$~output}}
scientific-computing case studies, this work analyzes requirements, design, and
implementation artifacts to identify recurring structures, shared domain
knowledge, and system-specific variation. These observations inform Drasil, an
extensible knowledge-centric framework implemented as lightweight
domain-specific languages in Haskell. Drasil represents concepts, definitions,
symbols, units, equations, relationships, and more as reusable chunks. Recipes
organize chunks for particular artifacts and audiences; deterministic generators
render requirements specifications, executable code in multiple languages, and
build files.

Implementing the case studies in Drasil provides observational evidence of
consistency by construction, explicit provenance, and deterministic
regeneration. Knowledge-level edits propagated across documentation and code.
Encoding also exposed missing definitions, inconsistent symbols, implicit unit
conversions, and undocumented assumptions in the original artifacts, suggesting
that knowledge capture serves as a diagnostic process that makes omissions and
contradictions easier to detect and localize. Reusing shared knowledge for two
related systems demonstrated that targeted changes can produce software-family
variants without duplicating common content.

These results do not constitute controlled measurements of productivity or
defect reduction. Drasil requires substantial up-front modelling, imposes an
onboarding cost, and currently works best for well-understood scientific
software. Within that scope, this thesis demonstrates the feasibility of
replacing separately maintained representations with explicit knowledge and
generated projections. Future priorities include improved authoring support,
broader knowledge representation, and controlled empirical validation.
